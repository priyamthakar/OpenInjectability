ABSTRACT
Purpose The force that has to be exerted on the plunger for
administering a given amount of fluid in a given time, has an
important influence on comfort for the subject and usability
for the administrator in intradermal drug delivery. The pur-
pose of this study is to model those forces that are subject-
independent, by linking needle and syringe geometry to the
force required for ejecting a given fluid at a given ejection rate.
Material and Methods We extend the well-known Hagen-
Poiseuille formula to predict pressure drop induced by a fluid
passing through a cylindrical body. The model investigates the
relation between the pressure drop in needles and the theo-
retic Hagen-Poiseuille prediction and is validated in fifteen
needles from 26G up to 33G suited for intradermal drug
delivery. We also provide a method to assess forces exerted
by operators in real world conditions.
Results The model is highly linear in each individual needle
with R-square values ranging from 75% up to 99.9%. Ten out
of fifteen needles exhibit R-square values above 99%. A proof-
of-concept for force assessment is provided by logging forces in
operators in real life conditions.
Conclusions The force assessment method and the model can
be used to pinpoint needle geometry for intradermal injection
devices, tuning comfort for subjects and usability for
operators.

INTRODUCTION
The skin represents the largest organ of the human body (1). It
consists of three layers: the epidermis, preventing chemicals
and micro-organisms entering the body, the dermis playing a
role in immunological surveillance, and the hypodermis or
sub-cutis that consists mainly of subcutaneous fat providing
insulation, restoring trauma and providing energy reserve
(2–4). The skin is considered of particular interest for drug
delivery due to its unique immunogenic properties and en-
hanced pharmacokinetics. To date vaccines are mainly ad-
ministered intramuscularly (IM) with syringe and needle.
Literature reports non-inferior immunogenic responses upon
intradermal (ID) delivery (5,6). Comparison of ID and SC
administration in type 1 diabetes showed a significant reduc-
tion of pain and superior pharmacokinetics. A more than 40%
reduction in onset and 24% reduction in offset time could
support closed loop therapy based on ID insulin administra-
tion (7,8).
Traditionally, ID injections are mainly achieved via the
Mantoux technique: a hypodermic needle is inserted almost
parallel, at 5–10 degrees, into the subject’s skin to assure that

the needle outlet is located in the dermal zone (9). This re-
quires training (10) and it is hard to exclude false-negative
injections without bleeding and bruising (11) and is considered
to be painful by the patient.
Micro-needle arrays are considered to be a promising tech-
nology for ID and trans-dermal drug delivery (12,13), but are
inherently vulnerable to damage caused by insertion forces or
forces for drug injection which might run up to 20 N or more
(14). This can result in a high failure rate in micro needle
based drug administration (15).
Single needle-syringe systems can reduce failure rate,
enhance repeatability of intradermal drug delivery and
omit regulatory challenges faced by micro needle arrays.
Single needle-syringe systems can facilitate testing and ap-
proval as an add-on to existing vaccination technology
(16). Adaptors that standardize ID injections offer the ad-
vantage to administer various drugs intra-dermally with a
single types of standard tuberculin syringe (17,18).
Solutions have been launched in the life-science market
to perform ID injections perpendicular to the subject’s skin
(12,16,19–22). Perpendicular ID injections are promising
new solutions since they allow targeting the dermis more
precise and standardized, compared to the Mantoux tech-
nique, independent of the user’s training experience. Also,
single needle-syringe perpendicular ID delivery systems
may drastically reduce pain compared to IM injections
and decrease anxiety due to the short needle length (23).
However the design of medical products is a complex pro-
cess that requires many iterations linking usability factors
with design requirements (24). In particular, the optimiza-
tion of single needle perpendicular ID injections requires
optimization for usability for the operator and comfort for
the subject.
A major usability factor is the force exerted by the oper-
ator to perform the injection (25). Injection rate, duration,
volume to be injected and drug viscosity are parameters
that directly relate to that force and hence usability of in-
jection devices (26–28). These parameters also relate to
comfort and acceptance of ID devices in subjects. In fact,
pain merely relies on infusion rate, rather than on the total
injected volume. Virtually painless injected volumes are
possible up to 1 ml at injection rates of 0.1 ml/min (29).
On the other hand, subjects do not want to hold an auto
injector on place for more than 15 s (30). Long injection
times might also lead to hazardous situations. Evaluation in
patients using an epinephrine auto injector revealed that
only 16% of observed injections where performed along
the manufacturer’s instructions and 76% of failures are
due to the patient’s inability to hold the device in place to
receive the required amount of substance (31).
There is a current the need to support the design of ID
injection devices that are placed perpendicular to the subject’s
skin, for optimized efficacy, usability and safety.
These desired outcomes depend on various parameters
and constraints.
Firstly, on one hand, the needle should be thin and sharp
enough, not to induce pain or inaccuracies when perforating
the subject’s skin. At the same time, a sharp bevel results in a
big outlet, increasing the risk of leakage, either exterior or into
the subcutis. Also, the thinner the needle, the more force is to
be exerted by operators for administering at the same rate.
Secondly, on the other hand, the needle diameter should be
big enough, to reduce injection time and forces required for
injection. At the same time, a bigger diameter results in a
bigger outlet, with similar risk on leakage. Bigger diameters
thus result in smaller bevels, enhancing risk for pain when the
needle perforates and inserts the subject’s skin. Also, pain
might increase with higher flow rate and higher viscosity.
Thirdly, the length of the needle is bound to similar trade-offs.
A fourth type of constraint is induced by new and promising
applications in cell therapy. Therapeutic cells are preferably
administered ID (32). As these drugs are highly viscous and
their efficacy is generally highly sensitive to shear stresses, it is
desirable to tune injection forces, ensuring functionality by
controlling shear stress and optimizing usability by controlling
injection forces.
Probably, the four type of constraints will conflict in the
design of new ID injection devices, and therefore be subject
to trade-off.
These potential conflicting specifications merely depend on
geometrical and mechanical specifications of needle and sy-
ringe, rather than on the physical and physiological charac-
teristics of subjects’ skin. Thus the question arises how to ac-
quire forces require to inject a given substance at a given
injection rate in function of needle and syringe geometry.
These data would allow tuning geometrical and physical
needle-syringe parameters in single needle perpendicular ID
injection devices, controlling injection rate, injection time and
forces in function of intended application, usage and target
group.
We provide a model for the injection force of ID injection
devices in function of syringe and needle geometry and drug
viscosity. We also provide a method to assess actual forces in
operators of site. Our model allows controlling injection rate
and injection time. This can be used to optimize the usability
in the design of new single needle ID injection devices.
MATERIALS AND METHODS
We provide a model for the hydrodynamic force from the
fluid going through the syringe and needle. The model is
constructed from hydrodynamic considerations and evaluated
in 15 commercially available needles suited for intradermal
injection, mounted in a commercially available 1 ml syringe in
a series of controlled experiments.

Controlled Experiment
A syringe adaptor was developed to administer fluid ID in a
standardized way perpendicular to the skin. The adaptor is
used in this study to control needle geometry. The adaptor is a
particular realization of a platform (38), on which a realm of
syringes with different dimensions and working principles can
be built with needle dimensions varying between 26 and 34 G
and protruding part of the needle, from the foot of the device
ranging from 0.8 mm up to 4 mm, injecting a pre-defined
fixed volume between 0.05cm3 and 0.3cm3. The basic config-
uration consists of a housing with a needle suited for intrader-
mal drug delivery, an activation mechanism and a skin pene-
tration mechanism.
Fifteen commercially available needles (BD, EXEL,
HSW and TSK) were selected to cover a variety of inner
diameters dnand lengths ln and implemented in the housing.
A regular syringe of 1 ml was used in the housing (HSW
Soft-Ject Low Dead Space Luer Lock). The syringe’s inner
diameter was assessed with a calliper with an accuracy of
0.02 mm. Each total needle length was assessed with the
same calliper. Bevel length was assessed by an optical mi-
croscope as a tolerance measure for needle length, denoted
z in Fig. 1, right.
Needle diameter dswas assessed through an optical micro-
scope too. Needle types and geometric specifications as pro-
vided by manufactures as well as assessed values are displayed
in Table I.
The housing with needle and syringe was mounted to a
force gauge (Electromatic ESM301) by dedicated designed
and 3D printed support parts. Injection speed vs was con-
trolled in the power bench. The power bench ensures a con-
stant displacement and thereby returns applied force as a

Force Assessment in Operators
The effect of the needle gauge on force exerted by opera-
tors using the VAX-ID™syringe adaptor was assessed.
The user scenario comprises the following steps: the oper-
ator fills the syringe by pulling up liquid from a vial; locks
the prefilled syringe without needle in the housing; places
the device on the receiver’s skin thereby activating the in-
jection mechanism and penetrating the skin; and injects the
fluid by a standard injection mechanism, that is by pushing
the plunger, narrowing the syringe’s reservoir and thus
ejecting the fluid. In a pilot pre-clinical test, only operators
are considered. Forces were assessed in 10 voluntary par-
ticipants. Participants were asked to eject different volumes
of demineralized water by pushing on the plunger of the
syringe mounted in the housing of the syringe adaptor. No
further instructions were given on how to perform the ejec-
tion. Fluid was ejected freely in the air without any injec-
tion in animal or human tissue, neither artificial substrate.
The housing was equipped with a 26G and a 32G needle.
A force -resistance sensor FSR-402 from Interlink was
mounted on top of the plunger. It has a sensitive diameter
of 13 mm and a sensing range from 0.2 N to 20 N with an
accuracy of 0.1 N. A connection part was designed and 3D
printed for solid mounting the sensor onto the top of the
plunger and for distributing force exerted by the operator
for stable and repeatable measurements. The set-up was
wired to an Arduino microcontroller for read-out by a
regular PC.
RESULTS
Needle and Syringe Parameters
The inner diameter of the syringe was 4.67 mm +/−
0.03 mm. Results of all needle measurement are compared
with given specifications in Table I.
The needle length of all evaluated needles was larger
than the provided length specifications: needles are
prolonged internally in the needle hub, from 5 up to
10 mm.
Specifications were converted from Inch to mm. Most
measured inner diameters of the needles correspond to the
given specifications, except for the 32G and 28G needles
that have larger actual diameters. The 32G-1mm needle
has a diameter of 0.127 mm with optical measurement
what is just in the specified tolerance limit (0.108 mm
+/−0.019 mm).

the 28G needle the measured diameter is just within

DISCUSSION
Difficult target groups such as children or elderly can benefit
from ID drug delivery due to decreased pain, safety and ease
of administration. In adults, a needle length of 1 mm is most
suited for ID injection at the forearm (39). The Mantoux
technique can target this depth by stretching the skin to insert
a needle with upward tip in the upmost dermal tissue. The
Mantoux technique dates from 1910 and to date is still used
for e.g. tuberculin and/or HIV infection testing (40) by
assessing the bleb visually for antigen reaction. It requires
trained medical staff to perform and is limited in needle ge-
ometry: relatively large needles of 27 Gauge cause bruising
and bleeding but reduce leakage and larger blebs, thereby
requiring more training. Relatively small needles of 30
Gauge have more risk of leakage and administering less effec-
tive dose (11). Current developments aim to standardize ID
injection to enhance reliability with solutions that inject sub-
stance perpendicular to the skin.
The total force for such ID drug administration consists of
four components: 1) the force of the needle penetrating the
subject’s skin (14), 2) force from tissue back-pressure due to
injection of substance (26), 3) force from internal mechanics,
e.g. in a classical syringe this only consists of friction between
the plunger and housing and 4) hydrodynamic force from the
fluid going through the syringe and needle. By direct acquisi-
tion of the third and fourth component and by assessing the
actual total force exerted by operators of site, an estimate of
the sum of first and second component can be obtained by
subtracting the third and fourth component from the actual
measurement.
Needle dimensions for ID administration are subject to a
trade-off. On one hand, thin needles and blunt beveled
needles are preferred for reliably targeting the dermis at
1 mm depth without leakage exterior and into to the subcutis.
Thin needles can induce the possibility of self-administration
(41). On the other hand, needle diameter should not be too
small since higher force required to inject at the same rate
might cause discomfort in operators and/or pain in subjects.
An upper limit of 30 N for injection force was recommended
for manual injection into air (27). Also, the formulation of
drug and in particular dynamic viscosity relates to bigger nee-
dle dimensions (28). Too high shear forces might also influ-
ence drug efficacy after administering through microneedles
(42).
Tapered microneedles are developed in a trade of between
enhanced flow rate and reduced infusion pain (43).

CONCLUSIONS
Needle and syringe geometry directly relates to the amount of
fluid that can be administered at a given time and with a given
force. The latter two parameters drastically influence comfort
of both subject and operator in intradermal drug administra-
tion. In the current development of small needles suited for
intradermal administration, much effort has gone into the
design of the needle’s shape.
We have presented a model to predict the force to eject
a given fluid through a given needle in function of injection
rate. The first part of that force consists of the friction
between plunger and syringe. The second part consists of
hydrodynamic forces caused by the fluid going through the
syringe and needle. The model extends the well-known
Hagen-Poiseuille equation used to predict pressure drop
induced by a fluid passing through a cylindrical body.
The model is highly linear in each individual needle with
R-square values ranging from 75% up to 99.9% and 2/3 of
these values above 99%. The model was shown to hold for
a 1.00 ml syringe equipped with needles with inner diam-
eters from 0.10 mm up to 0.27 mm and needle lengths
from 10.0 mm up to 50.0 mm and for Newtonian fluids
with viscosities from 1.0mPas up to 4.3mPas. We have
additionally presented a proof-of-concept for the system
to log injection forces in an in vivo setting. The system
and the present model can be used to pinpoint geometrical
and physical needle-syringe parameters in single needle
perpendicular ID injection devices, controlling injection
rate, viscosity, injection time and forces in function of
intended application, usage and target group.
